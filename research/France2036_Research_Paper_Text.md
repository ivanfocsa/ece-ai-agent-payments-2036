POLICY CAMPAIGN RESEAR CH / PROPOSED CLIENT: FRENCH MINISTRY OF THE ECONOMY AND FINANCE


Your AI Can Spend Your Money.
But Who Is in Control?
Autonomous AI agents and machine-to-machine payments | France | 2036–2040

Technology and 2036 scenario
Imagine a student in France asks an AI assistant to prepare a trip. The agent compares prices, gets
transport information and buys a small piece of travel data. It does not ask the user to open five
websites. It has permission to spend a limited amount of money to finish this task. In our 2036
scenario, agents could make these small digital purchases with each other, almost in real time. This is
a possible future, not a technology that is already common today.

There is already a technical starting point. Coinbase introduced x402 in 2025 as a way for websites
and APIs to request payments through the HTTP 402 “Payment Required” response (Reppel et al.,
2025). Cloudflare explains that software agents can receive a payment request, make a payment and
access a digital resource. Its documentation now describes x402 alongside another protocol, MPP
(Cloudflare, 2026). We are not claiming x402 will be the only standard in 2036. Our subject is a
broader future in which AI systems may pay for digital services with less human action.

Our proposed campaign is for the French Ministry of the Economy and Finance, as a hypothetical
academic client. Its audience is young adults aged 18–30, especially students, young professionals and
people already using AI tools. Our main question is simple: how can we keep the benefits without
giving an AI unlimited control of somebody’s money?

Potential benefits
First, payment could become more flexible. Instead of paying a monthly subscription, an agent could
buy only one API request, report or small data file. This pay-per-use model is one of the opportunities
described by x402’s developers (Reppel et al., 2025). It could help students and small businesses that
need occasional access rather than a complete subscription.

Second, it could save time. An assistant could compare services and pay for approved resources
during one task. For example, a small French company might use an AI agent to buy market
information and cloud computing within a €50 budget. Developers and small providers might also
find new customers when their services become easy for other software to buy (Cloudflare, 2026).
These are possible benefits, not guaranteed outcomes. An automated purchase is not useful if the
service is unreliable or the price is unclear.

There is no single winner. Users want convenience, companies want a good business model, and
public authorities want innovation that still protects people. Tracfin’s existing mission includes
combating money laundering and illicit financial flows (Ministère de l’Économie et des Finances, n.d.).
The AMF also explains that the MiCA transition for relevant crypto-asset providers in France ended on
1 July 2026 (Autorité des marchés financiers, 2026). Those rules concern existing activities; they do not
automatically solve every question about a future autonomous AI wallet.

Ethical risks and practical safeguards
Unauthorized spending. An agent could misunderstand a request or follow a malicious instruction
hidden in a website. A €0.10 payment could be acceptable, but thirty-five euros to an unknown service
might not be. We propose a fixed budget, merchant allow-lists, per-payment limits and human
approval for unusual payments. The user should also be able to stop the agent at any moment.

Fraud and unclear responsibility. A fake API might take the money without giving the promised data.
Users may not know whether to contact the seller, the wallet company or the AI provider. Our
proposal is to keep a readable receipt, verify the merchant, record why the agent paid and offer a
simple dispute process. The OECD’s accountability principle supports traceability and responsibility
across actors according to their roles (OECD, n.d.-a).

Privacy and surveillance. Payments can reveal interests, travel plans or habits. If the agent combines
this with other personal information, the result could be very sensitive. The CNIL explains that
personal data should be limited to what is needed and protected from the design stage (Commission
nationale de l’informatique et des libertés, n.d.). For 2036, we propose short retention periods, clear
permissions and private transaction information wherever possible.

Security and financial crime. If an attacker steals a wallet key, an agent may become a tool for fraud
or suspicious transfers. Strong key protection, spending limits, alerts and incident procedures matter.
Payment operators must also consider the existing French framework for financial supervision and
suspicious activities (Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution, n.d.; Ministère de l’Économie et
des Finances, n.d.).

Bias, exclusion and employment. An agent could prefer the same large merchants all the time, while
less well-known providers receive no attention. People without technical skills may find agent
permissions difficult to understand. Automation could also reduce some routine purchasing and
customer-support tasks. We would ask providers to test merchant selection for unfair patterns, offer
an easy manual option, and help employees adapt to new tasks. The OECD AI Principles highlight
fairness, inclusion and preparing people for labour-market change (OECD, n.d.-b).

Illegal or harmful services. An autonomous agent may find offers that should not be purchased,
including illegal services. The right response depends on the risk and the applicable law. In our
proposal, the wallet blocks clearly prohibited categories, requests human review for uncertain ones
and keeps a suitable audit trail. This is a policy idea for 2036, not a claim about a legal obligation
today.

French cultural and social values
We focus on France, not on comparing national stereotypes. In the French context, a strong argument
is that people should understand how their money is used and should keep a real choice. European
data-protection principles and French financial oversight give a practical basis for this discussion
(Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés, n.d.; Autorité des marchés financiers, 2026).

However, people in the same country may disagree. A startup founder may accept more automation
because every extra approval costs time. A student with a small budget may prefer to approve every
purchase. A privacy advocate may accept slower payments if less information is collected. These
differences show why “French values” should not be treated as one opinion. The important question is
whether people can choose the level of risk they accept.

Three ethical ways to judge the same payment
Utilitarianism asks whether total benefits are greater than total harms. If many people save time and
money, automation may be positive. But this argument becomes weaker when a system creates large
fraud losses or excludes vulnerable users. We should examine real outcomes, not only the promise of
efficiency.

Deontology looks at duties and rules. Even if a payment is convenient, an agent should not ignore
informed consent or a clear user limit. It should not hide a fee or deceive the user. In this view, some
rules should be respected even when breaking them would be faster.

A rights-based approach focuses on control, privacy and the possibility of challenging decisions. An AI
payment should not remove the user’s ability to understand, stop or dispute what happened. This is
close to the OECD emphasis on transparency, explainability and accountability (OECD, n.d.-a; OECD,
n.d.-c).
